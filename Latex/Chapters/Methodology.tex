\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

As already mentioned, missing data can arise in countless different ways,
and the choice of methodology depends on the nature of the missingness, the type of data,
and the specific goals of the analysis. We will focus on a subset
of cases, namely those that involve imputation of the missing values with
information drawn from the whole dataset, with the goal in mind of training a
downstream model that is as close as possible to the one that would have been
trained on the complete dataset. The setting is therefore an offline
reconstruction one: no causal constraint is imposed, and every method is free
to use the full sample, including observations that fall after the date being
filled.
Our choice of data, models, and evaluation methods will be guided
by these constraints. We will nevertheless mention when/how models should
be tweaked to solve adjacent problems.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

As our main baseline, we chose the most studied and reliable dataset in finance:
the end-of-day prices of the S\&P~500 constituents, fetched with yfinance from
1/1/2010 to 30/5/2026. This is not a series that suffers badly from missing
data; the only structural gaps are occasional circuit breaks and listings or
delistings from the index. To obtain a full dataset with no "real"
missing data, we removed each stock that contained at least one missing observation.
Furthermore, we selected a sample of 50 stocks from the index, stratified across industries;
this is a sensible choice because:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item with more than 400 stocks the covariance matrix $\bSigma$ quickly becomes
    unreliable (noisy covariances, skewed eigenvalues, unstable inversion) as the
    ratio of observations to assets falls;
    \item it is easier to verify that the reference data is actually correct;
    \item model training requires considerably less memory and computing time;
    \item a cross section of fifty assets is already wide enough to behave like a
    realistic multi-asset problem, with a market factor, industry structure and a
    substantial amount of diversifiable idiosyncratic risk, rather than a handful
    of series.
\end{enumerate}

This dataset will serve as the ground truth against which we will compare our results.
In parallel, we will modify it by inserting fake missing observations according to different
rules, as we define in the subsequent sections.

An important choice is whether to operate in the price space $P_t$ or in the log
return space $R_t$. There are advantages and disadvantages to each. The raw
dataset is prices, and in practice missing data mostly arrives as missing
prices; in most applications we also care slightly more about prices than
returns, since the former are what is actually bought and sold. But $P_t$ is
constrained to the positive half-line ($p_{t,k} > 0$) and is strongly level
dependent, which makes the series non-stationary and difficult for machine
learning algorithms to handle. We therefore work in $R_t$.

This choice carries an abstraction that is worth stating plainly, because the
motivation of Section~\ref{sec:motivation} is phrased in terms of prices while
the experiments below are not. The objects we delete and reconstruct are
entries of the return panel, not prices. Deleting a return is a stylised
representation of a missing price rather than a literal one: carrying a price
forward across a gap corresponds to a zero return over that gap, which the zero
imputation of Section~\ref{sec:zero} reproduces exactly, but a real stale price
is also followed by a catch-up return once quoting resumes, which absorbs the
move that was never recorded. Working in return space suppresses that catch-up
effect, so the results below should be read as statements about the return
panel a downstream model consumes, not as a full model of how stale prices
propagate.

\subsection{Missingness injection}
\label{sec:missingness}

We used three algorithms to simulate different missingness patterns:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sparse}: each scalar $r_{t,k}$ is missing with probability $r$, independent of the rest
    of the dataset. This is the easiest example of MCAR and can simulate a randomly corrupted dataset;
    \item \textbf{Contiguous gaps}: $n$ seed cells $r_{t,k}$ are drawn uniformly at
    random, and each seed is extended forward into a run
    $r_{t,k}, r_{t+1,k}, \dots, r_{t+L-1,k}$ of the same asset, whose length $L$ is
    drawn from an exponential distribution with mean $\ell$ and rounded down to a
    whole number of trading days. The second parameter of a gap level is therefore
    the mean run length $\ell$, measured in trading days, and not a decay rate; a
    level is written $(n, \ell)$ throughout. Runs are drawn independently and may
    overlap, so the realised share of missing cells is slightly below $n\ell/(TN)$.
    This is still an example of MCAR and simulates assets failing to trade because
    of random shortages of liquidity;
    \item \textbf{Stressed}: for each date $t$ we form the equally weighted average
    log return of the fifty sampled assets, $\bar{r}_t = N^{-1}\sum_k r_{t,k}$, which
    serves as our market proxy, and score the date by its square $\bar{r}_t^2$. This
    is what we mean by the index squared return: no external index series is used,
    the proxy is built from the panel itself. Dates whose score falls at or above
    the $p$ quantile of that score are declared stressed, and on those dates every
    entry is deleted independently with probability $h$, while on the remaining
    dates entries are deleted with probability $r$. A level is written $(r, h, p)$.
    This simulates the shortages of liquidity and the circuit breaks that occur when
    markets are volatile.

    The scoring statistic is computed on the complete panel, and the values it
    depends on are then among those hidden from the imputer. The market proxy is
    not retained as a separately observed series either: on a stressed date the
    imputer sees only the entries that survived, from which $\bar{r}_t$ cannot be
    recovered. Relative to the information set actually available to the imputation
    methods, the mechanism is therefore non-ignorable, and we treat it throughout as
    a stylised MNAR pattern. We note that the same construction would be MAR
    conditional on the score if that score were handed to the imputer as a fully
    observed covariate; it is the fact that it is not, rather than anything about
    the functional form, that makes the pattern non-ignorable here.
    
\end{enumerate}

\section{Models}
\label{sec:models}

\subsection{Deletion}

The baseline of complete-case analysis: every date containing at least one
missing value is dropped, and all downstream estimation runs on the surviving
complete rows. Under MCAR the retained dates are a random subsample of the
panel, so moment estimates remain consistent \citep{little2019}; the cost is
statistical efficiency, and in the cross-section it is severe. With sparse
missingness at rate $r$, a date survives with probability $(1-r)^N$: at
$r=0.05$ and $N=50$ assets, fewer than $8\%$ of the dates survive. Under the
stressed pattern the damage changes nature rather than size: the deleted
dates are exactly the turbulent ones, so the surviving subsample is calm by
construction and every risk measure computed from it is biased downwards, no
matter how much data is left.

\subsection{Zero imputation}
\label{sec:zero}

The simplest imputation that preserves the shape of the panel sets every missing
return to zero. Since daily log returns have a mean of essentially zero, this is
also the unconditional location estimate to within a negligible amount, and it is
exactly the reconstruction implied by carrying the last observed price forward
across the gap. The tables of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} report it under the name
Zero.

Its failure mode is predictable in closed form. Every second moment is damped:
under sparse missingness at rate $r$, a variance term survives whenever its own
entry is observed while a covariance term needs both entries at once, so
estimated volatilities scale by $\sqrt{1-r}$ and pairwise correlations by
$(1-r)$, shrinking the covariance matrix towards its diagonal. Under the stressed
pattern the distortion is stronger still, because the entries being replaced are
on average the largest ones. This is precisely the failure the structural metrics
of Section~\ref{sec:eval} are designed to expose.

\subsection{Low-rank matrix completion}

A few pervasive factors describe most of the covariance of equity returns,
so the return panel is close to a low-rank matrix plus idiosyncratic noise.
Matrix completion turns this observation into an imputation device: if the
underlying matrix has rank $q \ll N$, the missing entries can be recovered
from the observed ones -- exactly so, under conditions on the missingness
pattern and on how spread out the factors are \citep{candes2009}. We adopt
the iterative-SVD scheme of \citet{mazumder2010}: standardise each asset on
its observed entries, fill the missing cells with zeros, then alternate
between projecting the completed panel onto the set of rank-$q$ matrices
(truncated SVD) and restoring the observed entries, until the panel stops
changing. The fixed point fills every missing cell with a $q$-factor
reconstruction estimated on the fly, without ever specifying the factors.
The nuclear-norm variant of the same algorithm, which soft-thresholds the
singular values instead of truncating them, shrinks every imputed entry
towards zero and thereby inherits a milder version of the covariance damping
of zero imputation; we therefore keep the hard rank constraint, with $q$
small, in line with the number of pervasive equity factors.

\subsection{EM}

Our likelihood-based model treats the dates as i.i.d.\ draws
$r_t \sim \mathcal{N}(\bmu, \bSigma)$ and maximises the resulting
observed-data likelihood with the EM algorithm of \citet{dempster1977}.
Write $r_t = (r_{t,o}, r_{t,m})$ for the observed and missing parts of date
$t$ (the split changes from date to date). The E-step fills each missing
block with its conditional expectation,
\[
\E\left[r_{t,m} \mid r_{t,o}\right]
= \bmu_m + \bSigma_{mo}\,\bSigma_{oo}^{-1}\left(r_{t,o} - \bmu_o\right),
\]
while the M-step re-estimates $\bmu$ and $\bSigma$ from the completed panel,
adding back the conditional covariance
$\bSigma_{mm} - \bSigma_{mo}\bSigma_{oo}^{-1}\bSigma_{om}$ of the cells just
filled -- the correction that separates EM from naively iterating a
regression fill, which would understate the covariance. At convergence we
obtain the maximum likelihood estimate $(\hat{\bmu}, \hat{\bSigma})$ and, as
a by-product, imputations that are conditional expectations, and therefore the
fills that minimise the expected squared error given the observed part of the
date. In the implementation, dates sharing the same
missingness pattern are grouped together, so each pattern costs a single
linear solve per iteration. A small ridge term is added to the covariance and to
each observed block before it is inverted, which keeps the solves stable without
materially shrinking the estimate. Daily returns are of course heavier-tailed
than the Gaussian assumption wants; the same iteration extends to a multivariate
Student-$t$, whose E-step gains a per-date weight, decreasing in the Mahalanobis
distance of the observed block, that discounts extreme dates in the update of
$(\bmu, \bSigma)$ \citep{little1988, liu1995}. That robust variant is not used
here: all EM results reported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} come from the Gaussian
model described above, and the Student-$t$ extension is noted only as the natural
next refinement.

\subsection{$k$-nearest-neighbour imputation}

$k$-nearest-neighbour imputation, first popularised on gene-expression data
by \citet{troyanskaya2001}, is the donor-based, fully non-parametric member
of our set; the tables of Chapter~\ref{ch:results} report it as KNN. To fill date $t$, its distance to every other date is computed
as the Euclidean distance over the assets both dates observe, rescaled by
the number of co-observed assets so that sparser rows are not mechanically
favoured; the missing return $r_{t,i}$ is then the inverse-distance-weighted
average of $r_{s,i}$ over the $k$ closest dates $s$ that do observe asset
$i$. Nothing about the scheme is temporal: donors are selected by
cross-sectional similarity, wherever they sit in the sample. No
distributional assumption is needed, but the method degrades exactly where
donors become unreliable: under heavy missingness the distances rest on few
co-observed assets, and under the stressed pattern the extreme dates that
most need filling have no close, well-observed counterparts to borrow from.

\subsection{BRITS}

BRITS \citep{cao2018} is our recurrent neural representative. A window of
the panel is processed by two LSTMs, one per time direction. At each step
the hidden state is first decayed by a factor learned from the time elapsed
since each asset was last observed, so that the memory of a stale reading
fades, and two candidate fills are produced: a history-based estimate
regressed from the hidden state, and a cross-sectional estimate regressed
from the other assets at the same date. A learned gate blends the two, the
blend replaces the missing entries, and the completed vector is fed to the
next step, so the imputations themselves carry gradients through the
network. Training is self-supervised: the network learns to reconstruct the
entries that are observed, with a penalty tying the forward and backward
passes together, and the missing cells are then read out of the trained
network. No explicit model of the returns or of the missingness mechanism
is assumed.

\subsection{CSDI}

CSDI \citep{tashiro2021} adapts denoising diffusion models \citep{ho2020} to
imputation. In a diffusion model, data is progressively corrupted by
Gaussian noise over many small steps until only noise remains, and a network
is trained to undo one corruption step at a time; generation then amounts to
running the learned chain backwards from pure noise. CSDI makes this chain
conditional: the denoising network -- a transformer applying attention along
time and across assets \citep{vaswani2017} -- always sees the clean observed
entries while denoising the missing ones. Training is self-supervised,
hiding a random share of the observed cells and learning to denoise them
given the rest. At inference the chain starts from noise on the missing
cells, with the observed ones held fixed throughout, and each pass produces
one draw from the predictive distribution of the missing block given the
observed panel; the final imputation is the median over several draws. CSDI
is thus the method from which we explicitly generate multiple conditional draws:
the dispersion of its draws is a built-in estimate of the imputation uncertainty,
which no other model here exposes. EM also defines a probabilistic conditional
model, and could be sampled from in the same way, but we use it only through its
conditional mean.


\section{Evaluation methods}
\label{sec:eval}

Every experiment produces, for a given missingness pattern and level, an
imputed panel $\widehat{R} \in \R^{T \times N}$ to be compared against the
ground truth $R$ of Section~\ref{sec:data}. Let
$\mathcal{M} = \{(t,k) : r_{t,k} \text{ was deleted}\}$ denote the set of
imputed cells and $|\mathcal{M}|$ its cardinality. Since our stated goal is
not the recovery of individual values but of the panel a downstream model
would have been trained on, we score the imputations with two families of
metrics. \emph{Pointwise} metrics are computed on the imputed cells alone
-- the observed cells are returned untouched by every model, and including
them would only dilute the error -- and measure how close each filled value
is to the deleted one. \emph{Structural} metrics instead compare statistics
computed from the two full panels, and measure whether the objects that
downstream models actually consume (covariances, tail quantiles, marginal
distributions, temporal dependence) survive the imputation. The two families need
not agree: a method can be nearly optimal pointwise and still destroy the
second-moment structure, as zero imputation shows. Deletion is the
exception in what follows: it returns a panel with fewer rows, so the
pointwise metrics are not defined for it and the structural ones are
computed on the surviving subsample.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MAE}: the mean absolute error on the imputed cells,
    \[
    \mathrm{MAE}
    = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{(t,k) \in \mathcal{M}}
      \left| \hat{r}_{t,k} - r_{t,k} \right| ,
    \]
    expressed in return units and robust to occasional large misses.

    \item \textbf{RMSE}: the root mean squared error on the same cells,
    \[
    \mathrm{RMSE}
    = \left( \frac{1}{|\mathcal{M}|} \sum_{(t,k) \in \mathcal{M}}
      \left( \hat{r}_{t,k} - r_{t,k} \right)^2 \right)^{1/2} .
    \]
    This is the sample counterpart of the corresponding $L^2$ loss: the
    imputation minimising its expectation is the conditional expectation given
    the observed data, so RMSE rewards exactly the conditional means that the
    likelihood-based models construct, while penalising large errors
    quadratically.

    \item \textbf{Covariance error}: the relative Frobenius error of the
    sample covariance matrix,
    \[
    \mathrm{CovErr}
    = \frac{\lVert \widehat{\bSigma} - \bSigma \rVert_F}
           {\lVert \bSigma \rVert_F} ,
    \]
    where $\bSigma$ and $\widehat{\bSigma}$ are estimated from the true and
    the imputed panel respectively. This is our central structural metric: the
    covariance matrix is the structural object this thesis sets out to
    preserve, and the input of essentially every allocation and risk model
    built downstream. Being a relative Frobenius error, it weighs the large
    entries of $\bSigma$ most, so it is dominated by the variances and by the
    factor-driven covariances.

    \item \textbf{Value-at-Risk error}: for each asset the empirical VaR at
    level $\alpha = 0.05$ is the (sign-flipped) empirical $\alpha$-quantile
    of its return series, $\mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(k) = -\hat{q}_\alpha(R_k)$,
    and we report
    \[
    \mathrm{VaRErr}
    = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N}
      \left| \widehat{\mathrm{VaR}}_\alpha(k) - \mathrm{VaR}_\alpha(k) \right| .
    \]
    This probes the left tail beyond the second moment, the region that risk
    management actually regulates -- and precisely the region that the
    stressed pattern of Section~\ref{sec:missingness} empties out.

    \item \textbf{Wasserstein error}: the mean 1-Wasserstein distance
    between the per-asset marginal return distributions,
    \[
    \mathrm{WassErr}
    = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=1}^{N} W_1\!\left( \widehat{F}_k, F_k \right),
    \qquad
    W_1(F, G) = \int_{\R} \left| F(x) - G(x) \right| dx ,
    \]
    with $F_k$ and $\widehat{F}_k$ the empirical distribution functions of
    asset $k$ in the true and imputed panel. $W_1$ measures the minimal
    average displacement needed to transport one distribution onto the
    other; it compares the marginals in full, in return units, and detects
    shape distortions -- e.g.\ imputed values overly concentrated near zero
    -- that moment-based metrics summarise away.

    \item \textbf{Absolute-return ACF error}: every metric above is
    invariant to a permutation of the dates; the last two are not. Daily
    returns are themselves close to serially uncorrelated, but their
    absolute values are persistently and positively autocorrelated --
    volatility clustering, the most robust temporal stylised fact of asset
    returns \citep{cont2001}. Writing $\hat{\rho}_k(\cdot)$ for the sample
    autocorrelation at lag $k$, we compare the autocorrelation functions of
    the absolute returns up to a horizon $K$,
    \[
    \mathrm{AbsACFErr}
    = \frac{1}{NK} \sum_{j=1}^{N} \sum_{k=1}^{K}
      \left| \hat{\rho}_k\!\left(|\widehat{R}_j|\right)
           - \hat{\rho}_k\!\left(|R_j|\right) \right| ,
    \]
    with $K = 20$ throughout. An imputer that fills the panel with values
    that are individually plausible but temporally independent -- as any
    model that treats the dates as exchangeable must -- erases this
    dependence and is caught here and nowhere above.

    \item \textbf{Leverage error}: negative returns raise subsequent
    volatility more than positive ones of the same size, the leverage
    effect \citep{black1976}: the cross-correlation curve
    \[
    L_k = \mathrm{corr}\!\left( r_{t}, |r_{t+k}| \right),
    \qquad k = 1, \dots, K ,
    \]
    is negative at short lags. We report the mean absolute gap between the
    true and imputed curves, averaged over assets and lags as above. Unlike
    the previous metric, which is blind to sign, this one probes an
    \emph{asymmetric} dependence between the level of a return and the
    volatility that follows it, and thereby whether the imputed panel still
    distinguishes falling from rising markets.
\end{enumerate}

All seven metrics are computed by a single evaluation routine for every
model, missingness pattern and missingness level; the resulting tables are
collected and discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

\subsection{Downstream evaluations}
\label{sec:downstream}

The seven metrics above compare the imputed panel with the truth directly. A
complementary and in some ways more honest test asks a different question: if
we build several models on the imputed panel, how far is their output from each other?
We call this class of criteria downstream
evaluations. We fix a downstream model, fit it once on each imputed panel, and
compare the fitted objects across the imputation methods and against the one
fitted on the uncorrupted panel. The aim is to rank the imputation
methods against one another rather than against a naive baseline, so we keep
these results separate from the tables of Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. Two such
evaluations follow, the optimal portfolio and the factor structure of the
panel.

\subsubsection{Optimal portfolio}
\label{sec:portfolio}

The most classical downstream model in finance is mean-variance portfolio
selection \citep{markowitz1952}. From a return panel we estimate the mean
vector $\hat{\bmu}$ and the covariance matrix $\hat{\bSigma}$ and form the
fully invested tangency portfolio, the point of the efficient frontier with
the highest ratio of expected return to volatility:
\[
\hat{w} = \frac{\hat{\bSigma}^{-1}\hat{\bmu}}
               {\bone^\top \hat{\bSigma}^{-1}\hat{\bmu}},
\qquad
\bone^\top \hat{w} = 1 .
\]
Short positions are allowed, as in the unconstrained classical problem. The
covariance enters through its inverse, so any distortion the imputation leaves
in $\hat{\bSigma}$, the central structural object evaluated above, is carried
into the weights and often amplified there. The portfolio is in this sense a
direct stress test of the structural quality that the covariance error of
Section~\ref{sec:eval} measures in the aggregate.

In the implementation $\hat{\bSigma}$ is the plain sample covariance of the panel
supplied, and $\hat{\bSigma}^{-1}\hat{\bmu}$ is obtained by an ordinary linear
solve. No pseudoinverse, shrinkage or ridge regularisation is applied. For the
full-panel imputers this is unproblematic, since $T \gg N$ and the sample
covariance is comfortably of full rank. It is not unproblematic for complete-case
deletion at the heavier levels, where the surviving subsample has fewer dates
than assets: there the sample covariance is singular, no ordinary inverse exists,
and the linear solve returns a direction determined by numerical noise in the
near-zero eigenvalues. The deletion rows at those levels should accordingly be
read as a record of the breakdown, not as meaningful allocations, and we treat
them that way in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.

We compute $\hat{w}$ once for every imputed panel and once for the uncorrupted
panel. To judge an allocation we do not trust the panel it was estimated on: we
evaluate each weight vector on the true returns,
\[
r^{p}_t = r_t^\top \hat{w} ,
\]
and report the annualised mean and variance of the resulting series $r^{p}$,
together with its Sharpe ratio, annualising with $252$ trading days and taking a
zero risk-free rate. Since the components of $r_t$ are log returns, $r^p_t$ is a
weighted log-return series rather than the log return of a self-financing
portfolio, which would require weights applied to simple returns and rebalancing;
we use it as a linear portfolio-return proxy and nothing in what follows depends
on the difference.

Two points about what this exercise does and does not measure. First, the weights
and the returns they are evaluated on come from the same period, so the whole
comparison is in sample. The portfolio built on the true panel is the oracle: it
is the best in-sample allocation available, and no imputation can beat it. What
the exercise measures is reconstruction fidelity, how close an imputed panel
leads a mean-variance optimiser to the decision the complete panel would have
produced, and it is a diagnostic of that and not evidence about out-of-sample
investment performance. Second, we report the distance between the estimated and
the oracle weights as $\lVert \hat{w} - \hat{w}_{\text{True}} \rVert_1$, which we
call the $L^1$ weight distance; it is a measure of disagreement between two
allocations and not the gross exposure of either. A method that preserves the
second-moment structure recovers weights near the oracle and comparable realised
moments. One that distorts that structure, or that discards data as complete-case
deletion does, produces weights that look optimal on the panel it saw and behave
poorly on the real returns. The comparison is reported in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}, in a table of realised moments and a plot of the
realised series, kept apart from the metric tables above.

\subsubsection{Factor structure}
\label{sec:factors}

The portfolio reads the covariance through its inverse, so it measures the
distortion left by the imputation and the amplification of that distortion at
the same time. The second downstream model separates them. We take the
statistical factor model of the panel, that is the principal components of
$\hat{\bSigma}$, and compare the factors extracted from each imputed panel with
those of the uncorrupted one. Nothing is inverted here, so what the comparison
reports is the damage to the second moments alone.

Write the spectral decomposition of the estimated covariance as
\[
\hat{\bSigma} = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \hat\lambda_i\, \hat v_i \hat v_i^\top ,
\qquad
\hat\lambda_1 \ge \dots \ge \hat\lambda_N \ge 0 ,
\]
with orthonormal eigenvectors $\hat v_i$. The first $k$ of them, collected in
the $N \times k$ matrix $\hat V_k$, are the loadings of the $k$ leading
statistical factors, and in a panel of equity returns the first of these is the
market factor. We report two things. The first is the spectrum, through the
share of the total variance
\[
\hat\lambda_i \Big/ \sum_{j=1}^{N} \hat\lambda_j
\]
carried by each component, which says whether the imputation has concentrated
the panel into fewer factors than the data support or spread it over more. The
second is the position of the factor space itself. Let $V_k$ be the true
loadings and let $\theta_1 \le \dots \le \theta_k$ be the principal angles
between the two subspaces, the cosines of which are the singular values of
$V_k^\top \hat V_k$. These angles describe how the two $k$-dimensional subspaces
sit relative to one another as a whole. They are ordered alignment angles between
the two spans, obtained from a decomposition that recombines the eigenvectors
freely, so $\theta_i$ is not the error of the $i$-th principal component and
cannot be attached to any individual factor: $\theta_1$ measures the
best-aligned direction the two subspaces share and $\theta_k$ the worst aligned
one, whatever linear combinations of loadings those directions turn out to be.
This is exactly what makes the criterion invariant to the sign of each
$\hat v_i$ and to any reordering among factors of nearly equal variance, both of
which are arbitrary. We summarise the angles by
\[
D_k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{k}}
      \left( \sum_{i=1}^{k} \sin^2 \theta_i \right)^{1/2} \in [0,1] ,
\]
the root mean square of their sines, which is $0$ when the two factor spaces
coincide and $1$ when they are orthogonal. Dividing by $\sqrt{k}$ keeps the
values comparable across different numbers of factors. $D_k$ is therefore a
measure of how well the $k$-dimensional factor subspace is recovered as a whole,
and that is the only reading we give it.

The two downstream evaluations are connected by the same decomposition. Since
$\hat{\bSigma}^{-1} = \sum_i \hat\lambda_i^{-1} \hat v_i \hat v_i^\top$, the
inverse on which the portfolio of Section~\ref{sec:portfolio} depends weights
each direction by the reciprocal of its variance, so it is dominated by the
eigenvectors carrying the smallest eigenvalues. These are the directions
supported by the least data, and the ones an imputation has the least
information to recover. Comparing $D_k$ at a larger $k$ with $D_k$ at a smaller
one therefore says how much is lost once the deeper part of the spectrum is
included in the subspace to be recovered, which is what the portfolio implicitly
relies on. We report $D_1$, the recovery of the market direction, and $D_5$, the
recovery of the five-dimensional leading factor subspace, together with the
variance shares, and plot the full spectrum and angle profile, in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.
