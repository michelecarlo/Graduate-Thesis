INTRODUCTION
==============================================================================

SLIDE 1: TITLE (0:15)

Good morning. My name is Michele Carlo Fanelli, and my thesis, supervised by
Professor Corielli, is about filling the holes in financial data, and about how
we should judge whether the holes were filled well.


SLIDE 2: FINANCIAL DATA IS NEVER FULLY OBSERVED (1:00)

Financial data is never complete. Trading halts, feeds drop quotes, and illiquid
bonds can go unquoted for days.

The usual fixes are not neutral. Carrying the last price forward biases betas.
Dropping incomplete dates throws away much of the sample. Filling with a
constant damps variances and correlations. And new methods are almost always
judged pointwise: how close each filled cell is to the true value.

[Point to the four tiles] So the thesis asks four questions. Does the damage
depend on how much is missing, or on the mechanism that removed it? Do deep
learning imputers earn their cost? Is pointwise accuracy a good proxy for the
quality of the panel? And do the rankings survive when the panel is used for a
decision?

The aim, at the bottom, is to score every imputation twice: on reconstruction,
and on the model built on it, against the same model fitted on the complete
data.


==============================================================================
METHODOLOGY
==============================================================================

SLIDE 3: A CONTROLLED EXPERIMENT ON A COMPLETE EQUITY PANEL (0:45)

To answer these questions I need a setting where the truth is known. So I start
from a complete panel: fifty S&P 500 stocks, daily, from 2010 to 2026. That is
4125 days by 50 stocks of log returns. I use returns because prices are
non-stationary and level dependent.

[Move along the arrows] I delete entries under known rules, three mechanisms at
five levels, which gives 15 corrupted panels. Seven methods fill each one, so
105 imputed panels, each scored with seven metrics and two downstream models.

One point to keep in mind: this is offline reconstruction. Every method may use
the whole sample, including the dates after a gap.


SLIDE 4: THREE MECHANISMS, MATCHED ON THE SHARE OF MISSING CELLS (0:45)

These are the three mechanisms. On the left, sparse: each cell is deleted
independently, completely at random. In the middle, contiguous gaps: runs of
missing days within one stock, still at random.

On the right, stressed: deletions concentrate on the most volatile dates,
measured by the squared average return of the panel. This one is missing not at
random, because the imputer never sees the score that drove the deletion. You
can see the horizontal stripes: whole dates go missing together.

[Point to the table] The key design choice is here. The five levels are matched
on the expected share of missing cells, from 0.1 to 30 percent. So any
difference between patterns comes from the mechanism, not from the amount.


SLIDE 5: SEVEN METHODS, SCORED ON THE WHOLE PANEL (0:45)

I compare seven methods. Two baselines: deleting incomplete dates, and filling
with zero, which is what carrying a price forward does to returns. Three
classical estimators: nearest neighbours, low-rank completion and Gaussian EM.
And two deep models: BRITS, a bidirectional recurrent network, and CSDI, a
conditional diffusion model.

The one to watch is EM. Its fill, in the formula, is the conditional mean of
the missing returns given the stocks observed on the same day.

[Point to the right] Each panel is scored with seven metrics in four families,
from pointwise error to covariance and temporal dependence, and then with two
downstream models: a tangency portfolio and the factor structure.


==============================================================================
RESULTS
==============================================================================

SLIDE 6: POINTWISE ERRORS HIT A FLOOR AT ONCE, AND EM SITS ON IT (0:50)

The first result is about pointwise error, here the mean absolute error under
sparse missingness. What stands out is how flat the curves are.

Daily returns are almost serially uncorrelated, so nearly everything we can
know about a missing return comes from the other stocks on the same day. EM
extracts exactly that, and its error stays between 0.0071 and 0.0080 at every
level.

The five conditional methods differ only in the third decimal, so the extra
capacity of the deep models has little to work with. Only Zero, the dashed
line, stands apart. The ranking is stable across patterns: EM is rarely beaten,
and never by much.


SLIDE 7: ACCURATE CELLS DO NOT MAKE AN ACCURATE PANEL (0:45)

But accurate cells do not make an accurate panel. On the left is the
covariance error, on a log scale. EM's pointwise error stays flat, yet its
covariance error grows almost 40 times from the lightest level to the heaviest.
Pointwise metrics average over the filled cells; the covariance error
accumulates them over the whole panel.

[Point to the right chart] At 30 percent sparse missingness, Zero's MAE is only
1.5 times that of the best method, but its covariance error is 8.9 times.

Even among good methods the two views disagree. The best fill cell by cell is a
conditional mean, smoother than the return it replaces, so it damps the second
moment.


SLIDE 8: THE MECHANISM MATTERS MORE THAN THE AMOUNT (0:45)

This is EM's covariance error by mechanism, at matched shares. Sparse and gaps
lie almost on top of each other: with 49 other stocks observed on each date,
breaking the time axis costs a cross-sectional method very little.

The stressed pattern is different. It removes whole dates, so the conditioning
set is gone, and the returns it removes are the largest ones. At 30 percent the
covariance error is 0.66, more than ten times the 0.06 under random
missingness.

[Optional] BRITS falls behind where deletions are most concentrated, with an
MAE of 0.030 against EM's 0.013. At the heaviest level no method separates:
they all land between 0.648 and 0.663.


SLIDE 9: DOWNSTREAM I, THE PORTFOLIO AMPLIFIES THE DISTORTION (0:55)

Now the downstream models. The tangency portfolio inverts the covariance
matrix, so small distortions become large positions. The black line is the
oracle, fitted on the complete panel, with a Sharpe ratio of 1.636.

Under heavy random missingness, left and middle, the imputed portfolios take
on far more risk: variance rises from 0.049 to as much as 0.29. Under stressed
missingness, on the right, the imputers never see the volatility, and their
portfolios end at just over half of the oracle's cumulative return.

EM and Low-rank are never the worst. BRITS falls behind, for example with a
Sharpe of 1.30 against EM's 1.51 at one stressed level. This is an in-sample
diagnostic of fidelity, not a claim about investment performance.


SLIDE 10: DOWNSTREAM II, A ROBUST MARKET FACTOR, A FRAGILE SUBSPACE (0:55)

The second downstream model is the factor structure. The top row shows the
variance explained by each principal component; the bottom row, the angles
between the true and estimated factor subspaces.

The market factor is almost indestructible: its distance D1 stays below 0.026,
under one and a half degrees. The five-factor subspace is fragile: D5 ranges
from 0.14 to 0.39. Imputation also inflates the first factor, from 41.7 percent
of the variance to between 45.6 and 48.5 percent: the filled panel looks more
like its own factor model.

[Optional] Low-rank, which imposes a factor structure, has the worst
five-factor subspace at 10 of the 15 levels.

This links back to the portfolio. The inverse covariance puts most weight on
the trailing directions, which are exactly the ones recovered worst.


SLIDE 11: THE DEEP MODELS DID NOT EARN THEIR COST (0:35)

Finally, cost. Everything ran on a single Mac mini, on CPU. EM fills a panel in
about a second. BRITS needs just under an hour of training per panel, CSDI just
under four hours plus 24 minutes of sampling: about seventy hours of neural
training in total.

So CSDI matches EM at roughly four orders of magnitude more computation, and
BRITS falls behind at two stressed levels. In this setting, the deep models did
not earn their cost.


==============================================================================
CONCLUSION
==============================================================================

SLIDE 12: CONCLUSIONS (1:00)

To come back to the four questions. First, in this experiment it is the
mechanism, not the amount: under random missingness the methods hold up with
30 percent of the data gone, while under stressed missingness they break at far
smaller shares.

Second, the deep models did not earn their cost: no gain over EM, at four
orders of magnitude more computation. Third, pointwise accuracy is a poor
proxy: it misses the covariance damage and the inflated first factor. Fourth,
the rankings largely survive downstream, but the exceptions are informative:
Low-rank has accurate cells and a distorted factor subspace.

[Point to the box] So the message is simple: score an imputation against the
use it is put to.

The main limits are one panel, injected missingness and an offline setting.
The natural next step is causal imputation, using only the past.

Thank you. I am happy to take your questions.
